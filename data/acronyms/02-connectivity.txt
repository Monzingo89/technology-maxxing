usb|USB 2.0|Universal Serial Bus 2.0|Adds high-speed USB signaling up to 480 Mb/s.|Two-point-zero: remember 480.|USB-C connectors can carry only USB 2.0 data on some products.
usb|USB 3.2 Gen 1|Universal Serial Bus 3.2 Generation 1|Names the 5 Gb/s USB signaling tier formerly called USB 3.0.|Gen one gives five.|The historical labels USB 3.0 and USB 3.1 Gen 1 describe this same speed tier.
usb|USB 3.2 Gen 2|Universal Serial Bus 3.2 Generation 2|Names the 10 Gb/s USB signaling tier.|Gen two gives ten.|Usable file transfer rates are lower than raw signaling rates.
usb|USB 3.2 Gen 2x2|Universal Serial Bus 3.2 Generation 2 by 2|Uses two 10 Gb/s lanes for a 20 Gb/s USB tier.|Two lanes times ten makes twenty.|Both endpoints and the cable must support the mode.
usb|USB4|Universal Serial Bus 4|Defines a USB architecture that can tunnel multiple protocols over USB-C.|Four makes one shared road for several kinds of traffic.|USB4 does not by itself promise every optional feature or speed.
usb|USB4 v2|Universal Serial Bus 4 Version 2.0|Extends USB4 to support an 80 Gb/s signaling tier.|Version two doubles the earlier forty-gigabit tier.|Supported asymmetric modes and actual product speeds must be checked.
usb|USB-A|Universal Serial Bus Type-A|Names the familiar rectangular USB connector family.|A is the old rectangular host-shaped plug.|Type-A shape does not specify USB generation.
usb|USB-B|Universal Serial Bus Type-B|Names USB device connector families commonly seen on printers.|B is the boxy printer-side plug.|USB 2.0 and SuperSpeed Type-B connector shapes differ.
usb|USB-C|Universal Serial Bus Type-C|Defines a reversible connector used for data and power.|C can Connect either way up.|Not every USB-C port supports video, Thunderbolt, or high-wattage charging.
usb|USB PD|Universal Serial Bus Power Delivery|Negotiates supported power delivery between devices.|Power is Discussed before high power flows.|Maximum wattage depends on source, sink, cable, and supported profiles.
usb|PPS|Programmable Power Supply|Allows adjustable voltage and current within supported USB PD ranges.|Programmable power precisely supplies the phone.|PPS support is optional and must be present in compatible devices.
usb|HDMI|High-Definition Multimedia Interface|Carries digital video and audio between compatible devices.|High Definition plus Multimedia in one Interface.|A version label alone does not establish every optional capability.
usb|HDMI 2.1|High-Definition Multimedia Interface 2.1|Defines HDMI features including higher-bandwidth link modes.|Two-point-one expands the multimedia road.|Actual resolution and refresh support depend on device features and cable rating.
usb|HDMI 2.2|High-Definition Multimedia Interface 2.2|Defines an HDMI generation supporting up to 96 Gb/s link bandwidth.|Two-point-two widens the road again.|Specification availability does not mean every laptop implements it.
usb|DP|DisplayPort|Carries digital display and audio streams.|Display uses this Port.|DP capability and USB-C connector shape are different facts.
usb|DP 2.1b|DisplayPort 2.1b|Updates DisplayPort requirements and cable support.|Display Port two-point-one-b: check the bandwidth beside the badge.|A DP 2.1b label does not mean every product implements maximum UHBR rates.
usb|DVI|Digital Visual Interface|Connects displays using digital and, in some variants, analog signaling.|Digital Visual Images travel through DVI.|DVI-D, DVI-A, and DVI-I differ in supported signal types.
usb|VGA|Video Graphics Array|Names a legacy graphics standard associated with analog monitor connections.|Vintage Graphics use an Analog monitor connection.|VGA's common 15-pin connector is distinct from digital HDMI.

cable|PS/2|Personal System/2|Names an IBM computer family associated with legacy keyboard and mouse ports.|Personal System two left a round keyboard plug.|PS/2 is a product-family designation, not a USB protocol.
cable|IEEE 1394|Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers standard 1394|Defines a legacy high-speed serial interface also known as FireWire.|1394 means the FireWire family.|An adapter's plug fit does not guarantee protocol conversion.
cable|RJ-11|Registered Jack 11|Names a registered telephone wiring interface.|Eleven rings a telephone.|Registered jack names specify wiring arrangements, not just plastic shapes.
cable|RJ-45|Registered Jack 45|Is commonly used informally for the modular Ethernet connector.|Forty-five goes with the familiar Ethernet plug.|Typical twisted-pair Ethernet uses 8P8C connectors; the historical registered jack is a different wiring definition.
cable|8P8C|Eight Positions Eight Contacts|Describes a modular connector with eight positions and eight contacts.|Eight spaces and eight metal contacts.|8P8C describes connector construction, not the network protocol.
cable|UTP|Unshielded Twisted Pair|Carries signals over twisted copper pairs without overall shielding.|Unshielded but Twisted to reduce interference.|Cable category and installation quality still matter.
cable|STP cable|Shielded Twisted Pair|Uses shielding with twisted copper pairs.|Shielding protects the Twisted Pair.|STP can also mean Spanning Tree Protocol in networking.
cable|Cat 5e|Category 5 enhanced|Names an enhanced twisted-pair cabling category.|Five enhanced improves the old cable family.|Supported rates depend on standard, length, and channel condition.
cable|Cat 6|Category 6|Names a twisted-pair cabling category above Cat 5e.|Six steps up the cable ladder.|10GBASE-T reach on Cat 6 is more constrained than on Cat 6A.
cable|Cat 6A|Category 6 Augmented|Supports enhanced high-frequency twisted-pair performance.|A means Augmented for a stronger six.|A compliant channel supports 10GBASE-T to 100 meters.
cable|Cat 8|Category 8|Supports short high-frequency copper channels for data-center applications.|Eight is a short, fast data-center lane.|Category 8 channels have much shorter reach than 100-meter office channels.
cable|SMF|Single-Mode Fiber|Guides light in a single spatial mode for suitable optical links.|Single Mode for long-distance light.|Optics, wavelength, and connector compatibility must also match.
cable|MMF|Multimode Fiber|Guides multiple light modes, often for shorter optical links.|Many Modes share the fiber.|Do not assume multimode and single-mode optics are interchangeable.
cable|LC|Lucent Connector|Names a compact optical connector with a latch.|Little Connector is a memory cue for the Lucent Connector.|LC describes a connector, not fiber type or link speed.
cable|SC|Subscriber Connector|Names a push-pull square optical connector.|Square Click is the cue for Subscriber Connector.|SC and LC have different ferrule sizes and form factors.
cable|ST|Straight Tip|Names a bayonet-style optical connector.|Straight Tip twists into its bayonet lock.|Connector polishing and fiber type still need to match the application.
cable|MPO|Multi-fiber Push On|Connects multiple fibers in one connector.|Many Paths in One push.|Fiber count, polarity, and pin configuration matter.
cable|SFP|Small Form-factor Pluggable|Packages a removable network transceiver.|Small networking module Plugs into a port.|A matching cage does not guarantee that a transceiver is supported.
cable|QSFP|Quad Small Form-factor Pluggable|Packages a pluggable transceiver with four lanes.|Quad means four lanes in the module.|Lane speed depends on the QSFP generation and implementation.
wireless|Wi-Fi|Wi-Fi brand name|Identifies interoperable wireless LAN technology families.|Wi-Fi means wireless networking; learn the generation beside the name.|Wi-Fi is a brand name, not an expansion of Wireless Fidelity.
wireless|802.11a|IEEE 802.11a wireless LAN amendment|Defines a legacy OFDM wireless LAN technology in 5 GHz.|a takes the early five-gigahertz Airway.|Its nominal PHY rate is not application throughput.
wireless|802.11b|IEEE 802.11b wireless LAN amendment|Defines a legacy 2.4 GHz wireless LAN technology up to 11 Mb/s.|b is the old eleven-megabit Band.|Legacy low-rate clients can consume substantial airtime.
wireless|802.11g|IEEE 802.11g wireless LAN amendment|Brings OFDM operation up to 54 Mb/s into 2.4 GHz.|g gets faster in the same 2.4-gigahertz garden.|It shares spectrum with older 2.4 GHz devices.
wireless|Wi-Fi 4|Wi-Fi generation 4, based on IEEE 802.11n|Introduces high-throughput features including MIMO.|Four = n; multiple antennas share the work.|802.11n can operate in 2.4 or 5 GHz; products vary.
wireless|Wi-Fi 5|Wi-Fi generation 5, based on IEEE 802.11ac|Provides very-high-throughput Wi-Fi in 5 GHz.|Five stays on the five-gigahertz freeway.|802.11ac is not a 6 GHz standard.
wireless|Wi-Fi 6|Wi-Fi generation 6, based on IEEE 802.11ax|Improves efficiency using features such as OFDMA.|Six seats users efficiently into scheduled resources.|Wi-Fi 6 does not inherently mean 6 GHz support.
wireless|Wi-Fi 6E|Wi-Fi 6 Extended into 6 GHz|Extends Wi-Fi 6 operation into available 6 GHz spectrum.|E means Extended spectrum.|6 GHz availability and rules depend on the country.
wireless|Wi-Fi 7|Wi-Fi generation 7, based on IEEE 802.11be|Adds features including multi-link operation and wider channels.|Seven sends across several links.|320 MHz channels require appropriate spectrum, device support, and regional permission.
wireless|MIMO|Multiple Input Multiple Output|Uses multiple antenna paths to improve radio communication.|Multiple antennas In and Multiple antennas Out.|Spatial stream count and radio conditions affect real gains.
wireless|MU-MIMO|Multi-User Multiple Input Multiple Output|Uses spatial techniques to serve multiple users.|Multi-User means several clients share the antenna team.|Capabilities differ between Wi-Fi generations and devices.
wireless|OFDMA|Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access|Allocates frequency resources among multiple users.|One Frequency grid Divided Among users.|OFDMA differs from OFDM's underlying modulation structure.
wireless|MLO|Multi-Link Operation|Lets supported Wi-Fi devices use multiple links.|Multiple Links Operate together.|Actual simultaneous-link behavior depends on client and access-point capabilities.
wireless|SSID|Service Set Identifier|Names a wireless service set.|Service Set has an ID name.|An SSID is not a password or a unique hardware address.
wireless|BSSID|Basic Service Set Identifier|Identifies a basic service set, commonly using an AP radio MAC address.|Basic radio has its own Service Set ID.|Several BSSIDs may advertise the same SSID.
wireless|WPA2|Wi-Fi Protected Access 2|Defines a wireless security certification generation commonly using AES-CCMP.|Protected Access generation two.|Use supported secure configurations; WPA2 is not a cipher name.
wireless|WPA3|Wi-Fi Protected Access 3|Updates wireless security requirements including SAE for Personal mode.|Third-generation Protected Access upgrades the handshake.|Personal and Enterprise modes have different authentication models.
wireless|WEP|Wired Equivalent Privacy|Names an obsolete wireless encryption scheme.|Wired Equivalent was the promise; Weak Encryption is the warning cue.|WEP is broken and should not protect current networks.
wireless|WPS|Wi-Fi Protected Setup|Simplifies enrollment of wireless clients.|Push to Set up is the memory cue.|PIN-based enrollment has known security weaknesses.
wireless|NFC|Near Field Communication|Enables short-range contactless communication.|Near means the devices nearly touch.|Short range does not remove authentication or relay-attack concerns.
network|NIC|Network Interface Card|Provides a device's network interface.|A Network connection needs an Interface.|Modern interfaces may be integrated rather than removable cards.
network|MAC|Media Access Control|Identifies the data-link access sublayer and associated interface addresses.|Media Access has a local hardware-style address.|MAC addresses can be changed or randomized and are not proof of identity.
network|LAN|Local Area Network|Connects devices within a limited local area.|Local = nearby office or home.|A LAN may contain multiple logical networks and media types.
network|WAN|Wide Area Network|Connects networks over larger geographic areas.|Wide connects sites far apart.|A WAN is not necessarily the public Internet.
network|WLAN|Wireless Local Area Network|Provides local networking over radio.|Wireless replaces the local cable.|WLAN and cellular networks are different technology families.
network|VLAN|Virtual Local Area Network|Separates logical layer-2 broadcast domains.|Virtual walls divide a physical LAN.|Inter-VLAN traffic requires routing and appropriate policy.
network|STP|Spanning Tree Protocol|Prevents layer-2 switching loops by managing redundant paths.|A Spanning Tree avoids circular paths.|Here STP is a protocol, not shielded twisted-pair cable.
network|RSTP|Rapid Spanning Tree Protocol|Improves spanning-tree convergence.|Rapidly rebuild the loop-free Tree.|RSTP still needs correct topology and port configuration.
network|LACP|Link Aggregation Control Protocol|Negotiates compatible physical links into an aggregate.|Links Assemble under a Control Protocol.|A single flow may remain limited to one member link.
network|PoE|Power over Ethernet|Delivers electrical power over compatible Ethernet cabling.|Power rides on Ethernet.|Powered-device needs and the switch power budget must both be checked.
network|MTU|Maximum Transmission Unit|Limits the size of a network-layer packet on a link.|Maximum packet that fits Through the Unit.|Mismatched path MTUs can cause fragmentation or connectivity problems.
network|QoS|Quality of Service|Classifies and prioritizes traffic under a policy.|Quality service gives urgent traffic a better queue.|QoS cannot create bandwidth that does not exist.
network|SDN|Software-Defined Networking|Uses software control and abstraction for network behavior.|Software Defines the Network's rules.|A centralized controller does not remove the need for resilient forwarding.
network|SD-WAN|Software-Defined Wide Area Network|Applies policy-driven control across WAN connectivity.|Software Decides which Wide-Area road to use.|Underlay connectivity, routing, and security still matter.
network|SASE|Secure Access Service Edge|Combines networking and security capabilities in an edge-delivered architecture.|Secure Access moves Services to the Edge.|SASE is an architecture category, not a single protocol.

protocol|IPv6|Internet Protocol version 6|Uses 128-bit network addresses.|Version six stretches the address space.|IPv6 support does not automatically enable encryption.
protocol|TCP|Transmission Control Protocol|Provides a reliable ordered byte stream between endpoints.|Transmission is Checked and put in order.|TCP delivery does not guarantee that an application processed a transaction.
protocol|UDP|User Datagram Protocol|Sends datagrams without TCP-style delivery and ordering guarantees.|Unscheduled Datagrams go Promptly.|Applications can add their own reliability above UDP.
protocol|DNS|Domain Name System|Resolves names through a distributed naming system.|Domain Names become usable addresses and other records.|DNS serves more record types than host-to-address lookups.
protocol|DHCP|Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol|Leases network configuration to clients.|Dynamic Hosts Collect their Parameters.|DHCP and DNS have different roles.

protocol|ICMP|Internet Control Message Protocol|Carries IP control and error messages.|Internet Control Messages report network conditions.|Blocking all ICMP can break useful diagnostics and path behavior.
protocol|NAT|Network Address Translation|Rewrites network addresses as packets cross a boundary.|Network Addresses are Translated at the edge.|NAT alone is not a complete firewall policy.
protocol|PAT|Port Address Translation|Shares translated addresses by tracking transport port mappings.|Ports Allow many clients to share Translation.|Source-port mappings are different from physical connector ports.
protocol|HTTP|Hypertext Transfer Protocol|Transfers web resource representations and metadata.|Hypertext Travels through a Protocol.|Plain HTTP does not provide transport encryption.
protocol|HTTPS|Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure|Uses HTTP over a secure transport, commonly TLS.|HTTP plus Security for the trip.|HTTPS does not prove that a website's content is trustworthy.
protocol|FTP|File Transfer Protocol|Transfers files using separate control and data connections.|Files Travel through a legacy Protocol.|Plain FTP sends credentials and data without encryption.
protocol|SFTP|SSH File Transfer Protocol|Provides file operations over SSH.|Secure Shell carries File Transfers.|SFTP is distinct from FTPS, which is FTP protected by TLS.
protocol|SSH|Secure Shell|Provides encrypted remote access and related channels.|A Secure Shell protects remote commands.|Verify host keys instead of blindly accepting an unexpected replacement.
protocol|RDP|Remote Desktop Protocol|Provides remote graphical desktop connectivity.|Remote Desktop appears through a Protocol.|Exposing RDP directly to the Internet increases attack exposure.
protocol|SMB|Server Message Block|Provides network file and printer sharing.|Server Messages carry shared Blocks of work.|Legacy SMBv1 differs substantially from modern SMB versions.
protocol|SNMP|Simple Network Management Protocol|Retrieves management data and sends notifications.|Simple Network Managers Poll devices.|SNMPv3 security depends on the selected authentication and privacy mode.
protocol|NTP|Network Time Protocol|Synchronizes clocks over a network.|Network Time keeps clocks in step.|Correct time matters for logs, certificates, and ticket-based authentication.
